Games like Agar.io: what the genre became

Agar.io arrived in 2015 and invented a format: open a browser, be a small thing in a big arena, eat to grow, avoid being eaten. Everything with an .io in its name owes it something. A decade on, the games that inherited the idea have split into distinct branches — and knowing which branch you want saves you a lot of wasted clicks.

What Agar.io actually got right

The mechanics were almost insultingly simple: a circle that grows by absorbing smaller circles. What made it stick was the tension underneath. Getting big made you powerful and slow. Splitting to catch prey made you temporarily fragile. Every advantage came with a matching liability, so being ahead never felt safe.

Just as important was the format. No download, no account, no tutorial. You typed a name and you were in a live arena with strangers within seconds. That instant-in quality is the real inheritance — more than the eating, more than the circles.

The branches the genre split into

The cell branch — Agar.io and its clones
The original still runs, and a long tail of clones and private servers run alongside it. Splitting and feeding remain the deepest mechanics in the genre; competitive play involves genuine team coordination. The downside is that the skill ceiling now sits well above where most new players are willing to climb.
The worm branch — Slither.io and everything after
The biggest branch by far. Instead of absorbing smaller circles, you make rivals crash into your body, and they burst into collectable mass. It removed the split mechanic and replaced it with positioning and nerve. Easier to understand in ten seconds, which is why it outgrew its parent. See our full roundup of worm arenas.
The territory branch — Paper.io and relatives
Claiming area rather than eating mass. Your trail is your weakness and your land is your score. A slower, more spatial puzzle than either parent.
The shooter branch — Diep.io, Zombs, and the rest
Adds weapons and upgrade trees to the arena. Progression within a single match gives it a different rhythm — you're building toward something rather than simply surviving.

If you liked Agar.io specifically, here's what to try

  1. You liked the tension of being big and slow: the worm branch preserves this best. Size is directly a liability because your own body becomes the obstacle you have to steer around.
  2. You liked splitting and outplaying people mechanically: stay with the cell branch. Nothing else in the genre has replicated that particular depth.
  3. You liked the calm of just growing: Worms Zone and similar games lean into abundance and spectacle rather than knife-fighting. See our look at that end of the genre.
  4. You liked that it took five seconds to start: that's the common thread. Any game in this genre that puts a sign-up wall or a 30-second ad in front of you has lost the thing that made Agar.io matter.

What most .io games get wrong now

Two failures show up repeatedly, and both are worth checking before you invest time.

Fake opponents. Many arenas fill rooms with bots wearing player-style names. It guarantees a lively arena, which is genuinely good for a new player — but if the "multiplayer" is mostly scripted, the tension is theatre. Reputable games are upfront about AI backfill instead of hiding it.

Client-side collisions. If each player's own device judges whether they died, the game feels smooth until someone with a bad connection kills you through empty space. Server-decided collisions cost a little responsiveness and buy fairness. Most players never learn the term, but everyone feels the difference.

Where Wormifi sits

Wormifi is ours, so read this with that in mind. It's firmly in the worm branch, built around one rule inherited straight from Agar.io's design logic: nothing appears from nowhere. Cut a rival down and their mass drops as treasure exactly where they fell. Sprint and the size you burn scatters behind you, collectable by anyone chasing. Live rooms are decided by one server authority, so a cut means the same thing for every player in it. We use labelled AI backfill and say so rather than dressing bots as humans.

What we don't have: the split-and-feed depth of the cell branch, and the years of scale the big names carry. Our live multiplayer is an honest early arena, not a global network.

The quick pick

The original itch: Agar.io, still running. Easiest to love: Slither.io. Territory instead of eating: Paper.io. Guns and upgrades: Diep.io. Conserved mass and honest physics, free in a browser: Wormifi.
