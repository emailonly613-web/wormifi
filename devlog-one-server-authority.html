Devlog · architecture

Why one server decides every collision

The first real decision in a multiplayer worm game isn't art or controls. It's jurisprudence: when two players disagree about a collision, who is right?

The problem nobody sees until it kills them

Two worms cross. On your screen, you slipped past by a pixel. On the other player's screen — running 80 milliseconds behind yours — you didn't. Both screens are telling the truth about what they drew. A multiplayer game has to pick whose truth counts, and most casual arenas quietly pick "whoever's packet got processed in the luckiest order," which players experience as dying through empty water.

Our answer: the browser proposes, the server disposes

In Wormifi's live rooms, your browser sends intent — the direction you're steering, whether you're sprinting — and renders the world the server reports back. It never decides who was cut. The live badge in the corner isn't decoration: it appears only after the server has confirmed your session and delivered a fresh snapshot of the room. Until both happen, the game refuses to claim you're live, because you aren't.

What it costs

Honesty about the price: on a poor connection you will occasionally see correction — the server's version of events overruling what your browser guessed. We tuned the room to make that rare (the current public rooms run 32 actors with 15 snapshots per second), but we won't hide that it exists. The alternative is a game where a fast machine or a modified client can invent outcomes, and that game stops being worth winning.

What transfers to other games

If you're evaluating any worm arena, watch for the tell: do deaths near other players feel explainable? Our strategy guide assumes explainable deaths — geometry you can reason about. Games that can't promise that turn strategy back into superstition.
