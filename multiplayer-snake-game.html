Under the hood

What makes a multiplayer snake game fair

Two snakes cross. Two screens disagree about what happened. Everything you feel about a multiplayer snake game comes down to how it answers that.

The collision question

There are only two honest architectures. Client-trusting: each device reports its own outcomes — cheap, smooth, and quietly corruptible by lag or modified clients. Server-authoritative: one referee simulates the room and every browser renders its verdict — slightly corrected under bad connections, but the same truth for everyone. Wormifi's live rooms use the second, and the game refuses to show its LIVE badge until the server has confirmed your session and sent a fresh snapshot. The devlog tells that story.

The population question

The genre's open secret is bots presented as people. Bots aren't the sin — an arena needs bodies at 4am — the sin is the unlabeled kind. A fair game counts its humans out loud. Wormifi's room header shows both numbers separately, and every bot in the arena wears an AI tag on its name. When you evaluate any multiplayer snake game, find its human count. If you can't, you've found your answer.

The two-minute fairness test

Join, then watch three things: does a death near another snake feel explainable in geometry, or random? Is there a visible player count you believe? And when you boost, does it cost something you can see? Fair games answer all three within two minutes of play. Try the test on ours — friend rooms mean you can bring a witness with a room code.
